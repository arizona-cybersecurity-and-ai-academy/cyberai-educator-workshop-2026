Lesson Planning, Content Creation, Feedback and Grading

Day 1, Session 4 · 12:45-1:45 · 60 minutes

Author

Rob Honomichl, Sandra Leiterman and Ryan Straight

What this session covers

The practical hour. Building lesson materials, differentiating them, and drafting feedback, using AI for the parts of that work where it’s reliable.

This is the session where Session 2’s rule of thumb earns its keep. Nearly everything a teacher wants help with here is a task where you already have the content and you need it in a different shape: a rubric turned into student-facing language, a reading leveled for three different groups, a worksheet built from a text you chose, feedback drafted against work a student handed you. That’s the side of the line where these tools are strong.

Bring something real to work on. A unit you’re teaching this fall, a rubric you’re not happy with, a worksheet you’d like three versions of.

Session outline

Time Segment
~20 min Planning and content creation, worked live
~20 min Differentiation and accessibility of materials
~20 min Feedback and grading, and where the limits are

Key points

  • The tasks to hand over are the ones where you supply the substance and the tool changes the form. Ask where the content is coming from before you start.
  • Differentiation is the strongest case in the whole workshop. Producing four versions of one reading at four levels is work teachers rarely have time to do by hand.
  • Feedback drafting is real help. Grading isn’t the same task, and the difference matters both practically and ethically.
  • Whatever comes out has your name on it. Check it before it reaches a student.

Planning and content creation

The reliable pattern is to give the tool your material and ask for a transformation of it. Your unit objectives into a lesson sequence. Your chosen text into comprehension questions. Your rubric into a checklist a fourteen-year-old can actually use. Your slide notes into a handout.

The pattern that disappoints people is asking for the substance itself. “Write me a lesson on the water cycle” produces something generic that you then rewrite, and the rewriting costs about what writing it would have. “Here is my lesson on the water cycle, give me three exit-ticket questions at different difficulty levels” produces something you can use.

Both look like reasonable requests. The second one works because you brought the content.

Differentiation and accessibility

This is the strongest case in the workshop.

Producing the same reading at three levels, with a vocabulary list for the students who need one, and a version that works with a screen reader, is valuable and time-consuming. Most teachers do a fraction of it, not because they don’t want to but because the day doesn’t contain the hours. This is transformation work of the kind these tools handle well, and the source text is yours.

CautionTwo Cautions

Leveling a text changes it. Check that what came back still says what the original said, particularly with anything technical or historical, because simplification quietly drops qualifiers.

And a simplified text isn’t the same thing as an accommodation. An IEP specifies particular supports, and the fact that a tool can produce something adjacent to one doesn’t mean it satisfies it.

Feedback and grading

These are different tasks carrying different risks.

Drafting feedback

This one’s a good use. You’ve read the work, you know what you want to say, and you want it written out warmly and at length for thirty students. Give the tool the student’s work and the points you want made, then edit what comes back. The judgment stayed with you.

Assigning a grade

Different thing entirely. A score is a decision about a student, and these tools produce plausible-looking scores with no consistency between runs and no defensible account of how they got there. If a parent asks why their child got a 74, “the model said so” isn’t an answer any of us wants to give.

There’s also a straightforward privacy question. Pasting student work into a commercial tool sends it to a third party, and depending on your district and what’s identifiable in it, that might not be something you’re permitted to do. Check before, not after.
