AI Foundations: What Teachers Need to Know

Day 1, Session 2 · 9:30-10:30 · 60 minutes

Author

Ryan Straight and Kyle Cronin

What this session covers

The rest of the workshop assumes you know what these tools actually are, so this is the hour that puts that in place. What a large language model does mechanically, why that explains both what it’s good at and how it fails, and which of the things people commonly believe about them are wrong.

You don’t need a technical background for any of this. The goal is that by the end of the hour you can predict, before you try it, whether a given classroom task is one these tools will handle well.

Session outline

Time Segment
~15 min What a language model actually does

Key points

  • A language model predicts likely next words. Everything it does well and everything it gets wrong follows from that one fact.
  • Fluency and accuracy are separate properties. Confident, well-formed, and wrong is the normal failure, not an unusual one.
  • The reliable rule of thumb: these tools are strong when you supply the content and ask them to change its form, and weak when you ask them to supply the content.
  • Most of what people believe about how these tools learn, remember, and search is wrong in ways that matter for classroom use.

What a language model actually does

A large language model is a system that has been trained to predict what word comes next, across an enormous amount of text. That’s the whole mechanism. Everything else, the conversation, the essay, the lesson plan, is that prediction running over and over.

Be blunt about what that rules out. The model isn’t looking anything up. It has no database it consults and no notion of a source. When it produces a citation, it’s producing the sequence of words a citation tends to look like. That sequence is sometimes a real paper and sometimes isn’t, and the model has no way to tell the difference, because from the inside there’s no difference.

This is also why the output is so fluent. Fluency is what the training optimizes for. Accuracy comes along for the ride when the training text happened to be accurate and consistent, and it doesn’t when the text was thin, contested, or absent.

Where it’s strong, where it fails

Strong: you supply the content, it changes the form

Summarizing a document you paste in. Rewriting your paragraph at a sixth-grade reading level. Turning your rubric into a student-facing checklist. Translating your worksheet. Reformatting your notes into slides. Explaining your own text back to you in different terms.

In all of these the substance is already in front of the model. It isn’t being asked to know anything, only to restate. That’s the task the mechanism is good at, and it’s most of what a teacher needs.

Weak: you ask it to supply the content

Facts it has to recall. Citations. Anything numeric. Recent events. Anything where being almost right is the same as being wrong.

This is the failure that catches people. The model doesn’t know when it doesn’t know. It has no internal signal for the difference between a well-supported answer and a fabricated one, so it delivers both in the same confident register. A human expert hedges when they’re unsure. This won’t.

The rule of thumb

Before you use one of these tools for something, ask where the content is coming from. If it’s coming from you and the tool is changing its shape, you’re on solid ground. If it’s coming from the tool, you have to check it, and checking it often costs more than doing it yourself would have.

That single question predicts most of what you’ll experience in Sessions 4, 5 and 6.

Common misconceptions

“It searches the internet.” Usually not. Unless a tool explicitly says it’s browsing or retrieving, it’s generating from training, and the training has a cutoff date.

“It learns from our conversation.” Not in the way people mean. Some products have a memory feature that saves notes about you, but a plain chat doesn’t change the model. Nothing your students type is teaching it.

“It’ll get the math right, it’s a computer.” It’s a language model, not a calculator. Arithmetic is a sequence-prediction problem to it, and it approximates.

“It can tell you whether something was AI-written.” It can’t, and it will confidently say yes or no when asked. More on this in Session 3.

“It understands what it’s saying.” Set this one aside rather than argue it. For classroom purposes the question doesn’t need answering, because the behavior is the same either way, and the behavior is what you’re planning around.
