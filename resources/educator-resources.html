Educator Resource Guide

Cybersecurity and AI, for people who have to teach it on Monday

Everything here is free or free to try, already exists, and was built for people in your position. None of it requires a purchase order.

The sections answer different questions. Where do I get material I don’t have time to write? Where do students actually put their hands on something? What do I point the interested ones at? And when an administrator asks what standard this maps to, what do I say?

Curriculum you can take and use

Writing a unit from nothing is the slowest way to teach cybersecurity.

Teach Cyber is built specifically for secondary cybersecurity teachers, and it carries professional development alongside the curriculum. If you are teaching this for the first time, start here.

Cyber.org covers the full K-12 range and has a “Find Curricula” tool that filters to your grade band.

CLARK Center is a reviewed digital library of cybersecurity curriculum, searchable by topic, grade band, and how much class time you actually have. Lessons arrive with objectives and materials attached, so you can search for something that fits your week instead of building it. CLARK4Kids is the younger-learner arm, with units like Online Gaming Safety and Password Pros.

NCYTE, the National Cybersecurity Training and Education Center, is faculty-facing and skews toward the postsecondary end.

RING is a free online cybersecurity course for students and educators.

CodeHS runs a structured 6-12 cybersecurity sequence on its own platform, which suits a school already using CodeHS for CS.

Supplement to CSEC 2017, Foundational Cybersecurity Content and Instructional Guidance for Secondary and Postsecondary Cybersecurity Education (ACM, January 2025). The original CSEC 2017 guidelines were written for university degree programs. This supplement extends them to secondary education, which makes it the closest thing that exists to an authoritative answer to “what should a high school cybersecurity course cover?” You teach from CLARK. You cite this when someone asks you to justify what you chose to teach.

Hands-on environments

Reading about a network and breaking one are different activities, and the second is what students remember.

CyberHub is the Cyber Saguaros’ platform at the University of Arizona, described on the site as “the official cybersecurity lab, cyber warfare range, and eLearning platform.” Four things live there. The Crucible is a capture-the-flag environment with isolated practice lanes and live scoring. CyberLabs provides on-demand virtual environments for coursework and practice. The Forge is a malware analysis sandbox. And Clinic-in-a-Box is what the Day 2 afternoon tabletop runs on, an AI-assisted tool that walks a team through a cyber risk assessment against a mock client. If you want to run that exercise again with your own students, that is where it lives. Access requires an account, and whether that extends to educators outside the University of Arizona is a question for Paul Wagner.

Tannery Group: an instructor walkthrough is the companion to that session, and it was built during this workshop. You cannot get into the CiaB instructor system yet, so it walks the same ground by hand using a real client packet: the company, its five stakeholders, the scan results, the policy set, and the modelled attack scenarios. It is organized around a question rather than a document dump, which is what you can do with a client profile to teach students how to handle clients from a cybersecurity position. Every page also downloads as a PDF if you would rather hand it out than link to it.

US Cyber Range offers secure cloud-based environments for student practice.

Cyber Arena comes through the National Cybersecurity Teaching Academy. It was supplied without a link, so ask Paul Wagner for the access route.

CSforAR, Domain 5 runs professional development on networking and cybersecurity, including building networks with Raspberry Pi computers. Arkansas-specific, and the one item on this page most likely to already have a person attached to it in your district.

Competitions

Competitions do something a lesson can’t: they give a student a reason to keep going after the bell. All three run on their own calendars, so check when registration opens before you look at anything else.

CyberPatriot is the Air & Space Forces Association’s national youth cyber program and the most K-12-native option here. Teams compete to defend virtual networks, and the program is built for a teacher-coach who isn’t a security professional.

National Cyber League runs individual and team capture-the-flag competitions, pitched older than CyberPatriot.

SANS Holiday Hack Challenge is free, annual, and deliberately approachable. A student can try it alone over a break without you organizing anything, which makes it a cheap way to find out which of your students light up when handed a real puzzle.

AI in the classroom

MagicSchool is the teacher-facing tool used in these PD sessions for classifying data, exploring data pictures, and building out solutions.

Machine Learning for Kids lets students build and break small models in a browser, on data they choose themselves. Good for showing the limits of a model rather than describing them.

NC State AI Teaching Resources covers prompting basics for instructors.

Turnitin: guiding responsible AI prompts.

Databricks: AI literacy.

Both of those last two are vendor-authored. Useful, and read them knowing who wrote them.

Careers and work roles

IBM SkillsBuild has a student-facing catalog for exploring cybersecurity career paths.

NICE Framework is the NIST National Initiative for Cybersecurity Education framework, published through CISA. It describes cybersecurity work as roles, tasks, knowledge, and skills, and it is the vocabulary most American funding and pathway documents are written in.

DoD Cyber Workforce Framework is the Department of Defense’s own version. It matters if your students may head toward federal or defense-contractor work, and it does not line up cleanly with NICE.

One caution, because it will save you an argument later. These frameworks describe professional work at a level of detail K-12 standards do not, so a lesson does not cleanly “map to” a NICE work role no matter how much a form wants it to. Claim alignment where you can defend it and say so plainly where you can’t. A partial map you can defend will hold up. A complete one won’t survive the first person who checks it.

The CAE community

If your school is anywhere near a cybersecurity pathway conversation, this is where the national infrastructure lives.

CAE Community is the hub for the National Centers of Academic Excellence in Cybersecurity: professional development, working groups, and the people to ask. Membership costs nothing and it is the fastest route to a colleague who has already solved your problem.

CAE Resource Directory indexes what that community has produced, including labs, modules, outreach materials, and competition prep. It is broader and less curated than CLARK, so go there when you know roughly what you want but not what it is called.

NCAE-C Document Library holds the official designation documents and Knowledge Units. It is the primary source, which matters when a claim has to survive scrutiny.
