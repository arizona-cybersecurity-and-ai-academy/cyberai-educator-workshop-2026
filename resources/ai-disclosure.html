AI Disclosure Statements

Copy, paste, edit to fit

Most AI policies fail the same way. They’re written once, buried in a syllabus, and phrased so broadly that nobody, including the teacher, can say whether a given use was allowed.

What helps is a specific policy, stated where students will actually run into it, plus the habit of saying what you used yourself. Length has nothing to do with it.

Everything below is meant to be lifted and edited. Nothing here is legal advice or your district’s policy, and where the two conflict, your district wins.

For your syllabus

Pick the one that matches what you actually want, then say why. The reason is the part students remember.

AI is not permitted

For this course, all submitted work must be your own. Don't use generative AI tools
(ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, and anything like them) to produce text, code, or
images you turn in.

The reason is simple: this course is about building the skill, and you can't build it by
watching something else do it. If you're stuck, come to me. That's what I'm for.

If you're unsure whether a tool counts, ask before you use it, not after.

AI is permitted with disclosure

You may use generative AI tools in this course. If you do, tell me.

Add a short note at the end of anything you submit: which tool, what you asked it for,
and what you did with what it gave you. Two or three sentences is plenty.

Undisclosed AI use is an integrity violation. Disclosed AI use is just how you did the
work, and it won't cost you points.

AI is expected

This course treats AI as a tool you're learning to use well, which means using it, being
honest about it, and being able to defend what came out.

For every assignment, submit your prompts along with your work, and flag anything the tool
produced that you kept without changing. You're responsible for everything you turn in,
including whatever the tool got wrong.

For a specific assignment

Course-level policies get forgotten by week three. Restating the rule on the assignment itself is what actually works.

AI on this assignment: [allowed / not allowed / allowed for X but not Y]

If allowed, add your disclosure note at the end. If you're not sure whether something
counts, ask me before the due date.

A more specific version, for when the line falls inside the assignment rather than around it:

For this assignment you may use AI to brainstorm, outline, and check your grammar. Don't
use it to write the analysis, which is the part I'm actually grading.

If you used it, say so at the end: which tool, what for.

For students to use

Give them the words. Most students who don’t disclose aren’t hiding anything, they just don’t know what a disclosure is supposed to look like.

AI use on this assignment: I used [tool] to [what you asked it to do]. I [kept it as is /
rewrote it / used it as a starting point and changed most of it]. Everything else is mine.

Worked examples:

AI use: I used ChatGPT to help me brainstorm topics. I picked one from its list and wrote
the essay myself.
AI use: I used Claude to check my code for errors. It found two bugs and I fixed them. I
wrote the program.
AI use: I used Gemini to draft an outline and then rewrote most of it, because the order it
suggested didn't work for my argument.

For materials you made with AI

This is the one teachers skip, and it’s the one that carries the most weight. If you’re asking students to disclose, disclose.

Parts of this handout were drafted with AI assistance. I reviewed and edited everything
here, and I'm responsible for its accuracy.

A fuller version, for anything going beyond your own classroom:

AI contribution: [tool] was used to [draft / revise / generate examples / format]. All
content was reviewed and edited by [name], who is responsible for its accuracy. AI was not
used to [grade student work / make decisions about students / whatever your line is].

A note on the detection question

Don’t build your policy on AI detectors.

They produce false positives, those false positives don’t fall evenly (multilingual writers and students who write in a plain, formulaic style get flagged more often), and a student accused on a detector score has almost no way to prove a negative. A policy that depends on detection puts you in the position of prosecuting a case you can’t actually make.

Policies that hold up are built on process instead: drafts, in-class writing, conversations about the work, and disclosure norms that make honesty the easy path.

This gets covered in more depth in Day 1, Session 3.

Adapting these

Three things to check before you use any of the above.

Your district or school may already have a policy, and it outranks anything here. Check first.

Say why, not just what. “Don’t use AI” gets ignored. “Don’t use AI, because this assignment exists to build a skill you can’t get any other way” gets followed more often.

Name the tools you mean. “Generative AI” is abstract to a fourteen-year-old. “ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, or anything like them” is not.
