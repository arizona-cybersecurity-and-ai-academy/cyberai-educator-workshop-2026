Resources

Take these, edit them, use them

Two kinds of thing here. The first half is language you can lift for your own classroom — AI policies, disclosure statements, the words to hand students. The second half is everything free that already exists so you don’t have to build it: curriculum, student competitions, and the frameworks people will ask you to map to.

Nothing here is legal advice or your district’s policy, and where the two conflict, your district wins.

AI disclosure statements

Most AI policies fail the same way. They’re written once, buried in a syllabus, and phrased so broadly that nobody, including the teacher, can say whether a given use was allowed.

What helps is a specific policy, stated where students will actually run into it, plus the habit of saying what you used yourself. Length has nothing to do with it.

Everything below is meant to be lifted and edited.

For your syllabus

Pick the one that matches what you actually want, then say why. The reason is the part students remember.

AI is not permitted

For this course, all submitted work must be your own. Don't use generative AI tools
(ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, and anything like them) to produce text, code, or
images you turn in.

The reason is simple: this course is about building the skill, and you can't build it by
watching something else do it. If you're stuck, come to me. That's what I'm for.

If you're unsure whether a tool counts, ask before you use it, not after.

AI is permitted with disclosure

You may use generative AI tools in this course. If you do, tell me.

Add a short note at the end of anything you submit: which tool, what you asked it for,
and what you did with what it gave you. Two or three sentences is plenty.

Undisclosed AI use is an integrity violation. Disclosed AI use is just how you did the
work, and it won't cost you points.

AI is expected

This course treats AI as a tool you're learning to use well, which means using it, being
honest about it, and being able to defend what came out.

For every assignment, submit your prompts along with your work, and flag anything the tool
produced that you kept without changing. You're responsible for everything you turn in,
including whatever the tool got wrong.

For a specific assignment

Course-level policies get forgotten by week three. Restating the rule on the assignment itself is what actually works.

AI on this assignment: [allowed / not allowed / allowed for X but not Y]

If allowed, add your disclosure note at the end. If you're not sure whether something
counts, ask me before the due date.

A more specific version, for when the line falls inside the assignment rather than around it:

For this assignment you may use AI to brainstorm, outline, and check your grammar. Don't
use it to write the analysis, which is the part I'm actually grading.

If you used it, say so at the end: which tool, what for.

For students to use

Give them the words. Most students who don’t disclose aren’t hiding anything, they just don’t know what a disclosure is supposed to look like.

AI use on this assignment: I used [tool] to [what you asked it to do]. I [kept it as is /
rewrote it / used it as a starting point and changed most of it]. Everything else is mine.

Worked examples:

AI use: I used ChatGPT to help me brainstorm topics. I picked one from its list and wrote
the essay myself.
AI use: I used Claude to check my code for errors. It found two bugs and I fixed them. I
wrote the program.
AI use: I used Gemini to draft an outline and then rewrote most of it, because the order it
suggested didn't work for my argument.

For materials you made with AI

This is the one teachers skip, and it’s the one that carries the most weight. If you’re asking students to disclose, disclose.

Parts of this handout were drafted with AI assistance. I reviewed and edited everything
here, and I'm responsible for its accuracy.

A fuller version, for anything going beyond your own classroom:

AI contribution: [tool] was used to [draft / revise / generate examples / format]. All
content was reviewed and edited by [name], who is responsible for its accuracy. AI was not
used to [grade student work / make decisions about students / whatever your line is].

A note on the detection question

Don’t build your policy on AI detectors.

They produce false positives, those false positives don’t fall evenly (multilingual writers and students who write in a plain, formulaic style get flagged more often), and a student accused on a detector score has almost no way to prove a negative. A policy that depends on detection puts you in the position of prosecuting a case you can’t actually make.

Policies that hold up are built on process instead: drafts, in-class writing, conversations about the work, and disclosure norms that make honesty the easy path.

This gets covered in more depth in Day 1, Session 3.

Adapting these

Three things to check before you use any of the above.

Your district or school may already have a policy, and it outranks anything here. Check first.

Say why, not just what. “Don’t use AI” gets ignored. “Don’t use AI, because this assignment exists to build a skill you can’t get any other way” gets followed more often.

Name the tools you mean. “Generative AI” is abstract to a fourteen-year-old. “ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, or anything like them” is not.

Curriculum, competitions, and frameworks

Everything below is free, already exists, and was made for people in your position. None of it requires a purchase order.

Clinic-in-a-Box and the CyberHub

CyberHub is the Cyber Saguaros’ platform at the University of Arizona, described on the site as “the official cybersecurity lab, cyber warfare range, and eLearning platform.” Four things live there: The Crucible, a capture-the-flag environment with isolated practice lanes and live scoring; CyberLabs, on-demand virtual environments for coursework and practice; The Forge, a malware analysis sandbox; and Clinic-in-a-Box.

Clinic-in-a-Box is what the Day 2 afternoon tabletop runs on — an AI-assisted training tool that walks a team through a cyber risk assessment against a mock client. If you want to run that exercise again with your own students, this is where it lives.

Access requires an account. Whether that extends to educators outside the University of Arizona is a question for Paul Wagner rather than something this page can promise.

Curriculum you can take and use

Writing a unit from nothing is the slowest way to teach cybersecurity. Both of these exist so you don’t have to.

CLARK Center is a library of cybersecurity curriculum built by educators and reviewed before it goes in. You can search by topic, grade band, and the amount of class time you actually have. Lessons come with objectives and materials attached, so a unit that fits your week is a search rather than a build.

CAE Resource Directory is the browsable index of what the CAE community has produced — labs, modules, outreach materials, competition prep. It is broader and less curated than CLARK, which makes it the better place to go when you know roughly what you want but not what it is called.

Supplement to CSEC 2017, Foundational Cybersecurity Content and Instructional Guidance for Secondary and Postsecondary Cybersecurity Education (ACM, January 2025). The original CSEC 2017 guidelines were written for university degree programs. This supplement extends them to secondary education, which makes it the closest thing that exists to an authoritative answer to “what should a high school cybersecurity course actually cover?” You teach from CLARK; you cite this when someone asks you to justify what you chose to teach.

Competitions to point students at

Competitions do something a lesson can’t: they give a student a reason to keep going after the bell. All three run on their own calendars, so the thing to check first is when registration opens, not what the content is.

CyberPatriot is the Air & Space Forces Association’s national youth cyber program, and it is the most K-12-native option here. Teams compete at defending virtual networks, and the program is built expecting a teacher-coach who is not a security professional.

National Cyber League runs individual and team competitions in a capture-the-flag format. It skews older than CyberPatriot — strongest fit for high school students who have already been bitten by this.

SANS Holiday Hack Challenge is free, runs annually, and is deliberately approachable. It is the lowest-commitment way to find out which of your students light up when handed a real puzzle, because a student can try it alone over a break without you organizing anything.

The CAE community

If your school is anywhere near a cybersecurity pathway conversation, these are where the national infrastructure lives.

CAE Community is the hub for the National Centers of Academic Excellence in Cybersecurity — professional development, working groups, and the people to ask. Membership costs nothing and is the fastest route to a colleague who has already solved your problem.

NCAE-C Document Library holds the official designation documents and Knowledge Units. This is the primary source rather than a summary of one, which matters when a claim has to survive scrutiny.

Frameworks, when someone asks what this maps to

These two are how the field describes cybersecurity work itself. You will meet them the moment a district, a grant, or a pathway proposal asks you to justify what you teach.

NICE Framework — the NIST National Initiative for Cybersecurity Education framework, published through CISA. It describes cybersecurity work as roles, tasks, knowledge, and skills, and it is the vocabulary most funding and pathway documents in the United States are written in.

DoD Cyber Workforce Framework is the Department of Defense’s own version. It matters where students may head toward federal or defense-contractor work, and it does not line up cleanly with NICE.

One caution worth carrying, because it will save you an argument. These frameworks describe professional work at a level of detail that K-12 standards do not, so a lesson does not cleanly “map to” a NICE work role no matter how much a form wants it to. Claim alignment where you can defend it and say so plainly where you can’t. A defensible partial map holds up; a tidy complete one falls apart the first time someone checks it.
